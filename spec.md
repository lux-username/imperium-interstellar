# Imperium Interstellar — spec (design of record)

Changes only by deliberate decision. When a "maybe" in *Future direction* becomes real, it graduates out of this file into a Milestone with Issues.

## Goal

A solo, turn-based grand-strategy game in which the player governs a subsector on behalf of a distant Empire, where information travels only as fast as ships — so the player rules through delegation, standing orders, and a courier-borne inbox, never through direct control.

## Design pillars

These are the load-bearing ideas. A feature that undermines one of them is wrong even if it is fun.

1. **Information travels at the speed of ships.** There is no signal faster than a Jump. Everything the player sees is a *report*: an observation made somewhere, some time ago, carried here by a hull. The map is a belief state, not the world.
2. **You command through people.** Subordinates act for weeks or months on their own. Their competence, initiative, and loyalty are the gameplay. Appointments are the most consequential decisions in the game.
3. **Orders are postures, not programs.** Orders are a small set of mechanized types with a few parameters and a rendezvous — "patrol here, engage at favorable odds, then return to X" — not a scripting language.
4. **The inbox is the game.** A turn is: read what arrived, decide, write dispatches, advance a week. Packets cost money and determine how fast you can act.
5. **Isolation by mechanics.** The subsector's texture comes from 1977-era assumptions: low jump numbers, tech-level caps, charted lanes versus dark off-lane worlds, fuel risk, starport classes. Worlds are isolated from the point of view of the person in the tin can, not the god's-eye map.
6. **The player is a person with a location.** In Phase 1 that location is fixed: the desk at the subsector capital. The model treats the capital as the *delivery address*, so mobility can be added later without rewriting the information system.
7. **Original text, original setting.** Mechanics are inspired by 1977-era 2d6 science-fiction RPG design as analysed by the *Tales to Astound!* "Out of the Box" essays. No rules text is copied; no trademarks or third-party setting names appear anywhere in the game.

## Requirements

### Setting assumptions (inherited mechanics)

- **Subsector**: an 8×10 hex grid; each hex is one parsec. Roughly 30–40 worlds (about 40% hex density — deliberately sparser than the ~50% rules-default, so clusters exist).
- **Jump**: a ship with Jump-N can move up to N parsecs in one jump. Every jump takes one week regardless of distance. In-system manoeuvre is absorbed into the week.
- **Jump ratings**: J-1 and J-2 are common, J-3 uncommon, J-4 rare and expensive. The shipyard world's tech level caps what can be built.
- **Starports**: classes A–E and X. A/B: refined fuel, shipyards, regular traffic. C: unrefined fuel only. D/E: little or no fuel. X: nothing. Jumping on unrefined fuel risks drive damage and misjump.
- **Charted lanes**: regular commercial and packet traffic runs only along charted lanes between A/B (sometimes C) ports. Off-lane jumps require a capable navigator and computer, and no scheduled ship ever makes them. Off-lane worlds see a hull only when someone sends one deliberately — and when one calls, it carries away whatever mail has been lying at the port with no way home. (Phase 1a lets any hull the desk sends jump off-lane within its rating; fuel risk and navigator quality are Phase 1b.)
- **World profile**: starport, size, atmosphere, hydrographics, population, government, law level, tech level — generated by our own 2d6 procedures producing a compact profile string.
- **Communication equals travel.** Couriers are ships. Packet ships run lanes on schedules; anything else is a ship someone chose to send.
- **Scout ships**: tiny two-person hulls, self-sufficient for months, J-2, no cargo and no fight in them — but exceptionally good at *not* being caught: a scout that meets a hostile gets a large bonus to break off and jump out. Every side has them. They are the cheapest way to look at a world, and the presence of *someone else's* scout in a system is itself news.

### Core loop (one turn = one week)

1. **Inbox.** Dispatches that arrived this week are presented, each stamped with *origin date*, *origin location*, and *arrival date*.
2. **Update.** Map, roster, and ledger re-render from the player's latest reports. Every item shows its age ("as of 9 weeks ago").
3. **Act.** The player issues orders (which become outgoing dispatches on couriers), makes appointments, sets budgets, and replies to the Empire.
4. **Advance week.** The simulation resolves every entity in the subsector using *its own* knowledge and standing orders; ships move; couriers and reports advance along their routes; events fire; the player's outgoing dispatches begin travelling.

### Information model

- **Ground truth vs. belief.** The simulation holds the true state. The player's view is a set of *reports*: snapshots of an entity as observed at (location, date), delivered at (capital, later date). The map renders the newest report per entity and its age.
- **Events are the unit of news.** The simulation records *events* — an occurrence at a place on a week, with a kind, a subject, a valence (good or bad for whoever hears it; *neutral* for routine traffic nobody writes home about) and a severity: unrest stepped, garrison lost, an unknown hull seen, a governor changed, a raid, a dispatch received. A governor's letter is a snapshot of their world plus the events they *chose to mention*; a rumour is a degraded event; the Council's picture is a fold over the events that reached it. One primitive feeds all three levels.
- **Every report names its channel.** `official` (a governor's or commander's own letter), `agent` (one of the player's agents on the spot), `merchant` (a trader's word at a port), `docks` (talk nobody will put a name to). The channel is what the player legitimately knows about a report's provenance; there is *no* reliability score. Distortion is baked into the content when the report is written — by the observer's traits, or by the hops a rumour has made — and never recorded on the report, so the UI cannot render "60% true".
- **Report propagation.** An observation made at world X on week D leaves X on the next departing hull bound toward the capital (a scheduled packet on a lane, or whatever ship happens to go). Arrival = D + waiting time + transit weeks. Off-lane worlds may not report for months, or ever.
- **Dispatch propagation** (player → subordinate). Same rules in reverse. A dispatch addressed to a ship is sent to an *address* — a world or rally point. If the ship is not there when the courier arrives, the dispatch is held at the address until the ship shows up or a recall expires. **Orders go to places, not hulls**: the desk writes to where a ship *will be* — its last known port, its destination, or its rendezvous — and the port holds the letter. Routine orders ride the packets; where no packet goes, or when it would land sooner, a hull in port at the capital is dispatched to carry them and return, and the letter is routed along that hull's run. There is no way to nest orders inside orders: the courier *is* the mail. (Historical model: the Post Office packets and the Navy's advice boats, side by side.)
- **Who carries what.** A scheduled packet takes only mail whose next leg is its jump. Any other hull leaving a port takes a *copy* of every report waiting there for the capital (the original waits for the packet; whichever copy arrives first is read, the rest are discarded) and takes outright any mail with no scheduled way home — but only when its own run calls at the destination or at a port on lanes that reach it. A hull bound the wrong way leaves the letters where they are. **A packet or courier that is captured destroys its mail**: nothing aboard is read by the captor, everything is lost, and the desk learns of the loss only by a report of the capture. (Later: packet lines run only between controlled ports, the desk may establish new lines, and pirates or enemy patrols on a lane disrupt both mail and rumour — Phase 1b/1c issues.)
- **Rumour propagation.** Merchants carry news along the lanes. A rumour is a degraded report of an event — vague on date and place, mostly but not always reliable, sometimes about things no official observer saw — that hops lane to lane with commercial traffic, slower than packets, and reaches every A/B port in time, including ports outside the subsector. Its channel is `merchant` or `docks`, and its observed week and place may be off by a few weeks and a world. Rumour flows both ways: it is how the Council hears of the subsector beyond what the player chooses to tell it, and how the player hears of the Council's mood and of corners of the subsector no official report has come from — a world that has gone quiet, a battle nobody survived to report. **Rumours arrive in their own section of the inbox** ("what you hear"), never mixed with official mail, so the player always knows which pile a claim came from — and **rumour never touches the map**: talk is not knowledge, so no marker, sighting or dossier fact rests on it. The dossier lists official reports first and what the docks say beneath.
- **Subordinate knowledge.** Every acting entity (governor, ship commander, rival polity) has its own belief state and acts on it. Phase 1 may simplify NPC decision inputs, but a subordinate's *report to the player* is always what that subordinate observed, coloured by their traits. The Warlord is the exception to the simplification: his belief state is tracked exactly as the player's is — built only from what reaches his capital — and he sends scouts to fill it.
- **Scouts are the agents.** There are no separate agent characters: the watching is done by **scout ships** — tiny two-person hulls with a large bonus to break off. A scout sent to *look* at a world writes an ordinary commander's letter on arrival; a scout sent to **watch** for N weeks lies off the world and, at the end of the stay, writes the one **fully accurate** report in the game, on the `agent` channel: the world's true state, every hull that called while she watched (when it arrived, when it left), and any enemy scout that came to look — then she heads for her rendezvous. That report is the player's only unfiltered truth, and there are only a couple of scouts; they do not sharpen rumours or judge anyone's honesty, they simply see. A watching scout is a hull in the system: a governor may write home about the strange hull off the port, and warships arriving can chase her off.
- **Player location.** Stored as a location entity. Phase 1: always the capital. The capital's `acting_governor` slot exists from day one and holds the player.

### Orders

Few, mechanizable, with a rendezvous. Each order type is a data record, not code.

**Ship orders**
- *Move* to X by lane (or off-lane if capable), then hold / return to Y.
- *Patrol* world-or-region for N weeks with engagement posture ∈ {never, only overwhelming, favourable, even, always}; then return to rally Y.
- *Transport* troops, cargo, or personnel from A to B.
- *Courier*: carry dispatches along a route and return.
- *Scout* a world: go there, observe for a week, come back and report (the commander's report is `official` and coloured by their traits like any other). Off-lane worlds need a capable hull. Every side's scouts use this order.

A commander writes home from **every friendly port on the lanes** their hull calls at — by the next packet — so the desk can follow a hull from port to port, and again when a patrol ends. Off the lanes they write only where the order was taking them, and carry the letter to the next port with a lane home.

**Standing orders** attached to any ship: on completion → rally point; on contact with faction F → posture; on damage beyond threshold → retreat to nearest friendly A/B port; on unexpected situation → hold and send a courier.

**Governor orders**: extractive taxation (an emergency measure; see *Economy*), garrison priority, infrastructure (starport upgrade, lane charting), unrest posture, recruitment. Reporting is not an order: governors write when something happens, filtered by their traits (see *Characters → Effects*); the player's letter asking for a report forces one, still coloured.

**Scout orders**: *Look* (a week and a letter) or *Watch* a world for N weeks (see *Information model → Scouts are the agents*). Subversion and protection of assets are Phase 2.

### Characters

Governors, ship commanders and commodores, deputies, and the Empire's envoy.

- **Attributes**: competence (administrative / naval / diplomatic), initiative (cautious ↔ bold), loyalty (to the player / to the Empire / to self), ambition, and a small set of quirks.
- **Effects**: traits bias how orders are interpreted (a bold commander shifts engagement posture one step up; a cautious one retreats early), report honesty and frequency, likelihood of exceeding orders, and handling of unrest.
- **What they choose to tell you.** A governor or commander writes home when an event crosses a threshold, not on a schedule. For unrest the threshold is a change of *mood* — content (0–1), neutral (2–5), hostile (6–10) — or reaching either end of the scale, reported once per visit; a point of drift within a mood is not news, though the world's unrest as the governor sees it rides in every letter they do send. Good news always goes out — it costs nothing and looks good — and a new governor introduces themselves. Routine traffic is never worth a letter. Each piece of bad news gets a *disclosure* roll (2d6 against a threshold): severity pushes it up; loyalty to self pushes it down for anything that implicates the writer; boldness pushes "I need help" down and "handled" up, so a bold governor sits on rising unrest until it is too late while a cautious one writes early and asks for troops. Colouring is applied when the letter is written: a self-loyal governor understates unrest and omits the pirate hulls in port if theirs is a haven. A slow routine "all quiet" letter (every 8–12 weeks) means silence is itself a signal. The player's own monthly report to the Council is selective in exactly the same way one level up.
- **Relationships**: patronage, rivalry, faction ties. Careers: they age, die, get promoted, resign, defect.
- **Learning them**: the player only ever knows a character through their reports and outcomes — and, for officers physically at the capital (awaiting assignment, or because their ship is in port), through **conversation**: the player may speak with one such officer per week, and receives a **written impression** — a boast, an evasion, a slip — from which personality and loyalty must be inferred. (If the cast grows too large for impressions to be enough, a direct trait read may be added.) Dossiers accumulate; they are never omniscient, and a self-loyal officer has every reason to present as a loyal one.

### The Empire (delegation upward)

The imperial capital lies outside the subsector, roughly 12–16 weeks round trip. It sends directives, quotas (tax, ship levies), and judgement — all based on reports that were months old when read. It may reward, censure, recall, or replace the player. The player's standing with the Empire and the subsector's stability and prosperity are the game's scoring axes. Losing means recall in disgrace, or losing the subsector.

### Threats and events

Piracy on lanes; revolt on isolated worlds; a rival polity across the border; a governor's secession; misjump; plague, famine, disaster. Every event produces reports that reach the player late, and sometimes contradictory reports from different witnesses.

### Economy (kept light)

Treasury at the capital. Ships cost upkeep; couriers cost per packet. Phase 1 needs only enough economy to make packets and ships scarce.

**The Empire taxes trade, not planets.** It does not govern worlds and could not: occupying a planet with ships this small is out of the question. What it holds is **spaceport basing rights** and the **fees on interstellar trade**, which the imperial trade guild — holding the monopoly on carriage between the stars — pays richly for. So revenue is not a per-world yield from population. It is collected thus: **every world joined to the capital by a trade route sends funds along that route**, representing merchants plying the lanes and paying their fees at each spaceport. The money travels at a merchant's pace, as mail (*specie*), and is taken if a pirate or an enemy holds the route; a blocked route sends nothing until it is cleared. **Extractive taxation** of a world's own population is an order a governor can be given, but it is an emergency measure: it raises unrest sharply, and a world pushed into revolt closes its port and stops the trade fees as well. **Worlds off the trade routes are financial liabilities** — they cost a garrison and return nothing — and the Empire holds them anyway, because a world let go becomes a pirate haven or a Warlord's base.

### Combat (abstract)

Resolved by the simulation when hostile forces share a system: strength, posture, commander traits, and dice produce damage, destruction, or retreat. No tactical layer. The player learns of a battle from the commander's own after-action report, which may be self-serving.

**Fuel and repair.** Every jump costs a tank; a hull's class says how many she carries (warships four, scouts six). Only a port of **class B or better that is open to her side** fills them — for a pirate, a haven she knows of that class — and only a port of **class C or better** repairs her, a point a week. A hull down to her last jump goes for fuel before anything else, to her rendezvous if it will serve, and one with none sits where she is. Packets burn nothing: the lanes keep them fuelled. Fuel is spent on jumps only.

**Ports have guns.** A starport's batteries count as strength for any side docked there — A 3, B 2, C 1, nothing below — both in weighing the odds and in the exchange, and docked hulls stand and fight from under them rather than running. The port protects what lies under it, not the approaches: a hull that came out of jump this week is in the open, and a raider lying off a port weighs the arrivals she can catch, counting the docked hulls and the guns only if those would sortie to meet her. Having taken what she can in the open, she may go for the port too if the odds against hulls-and-guns suit her posture. So a lone raider leaves a packet under a B port alone and robs one under a D port; two together will try a B port and get shot at for it; and no port is a wall.

**Ground combat** uses one attrition model wherever two forces contest a world — a garrison against a revolt, the player's troops against the Warlord's, or his against the player's. Each week the contest continues, each side has a chance of losing a detachment's worth of strength to ambushes and assaults; the odds turn on relative strength and terrain (the world's profile), and the contest ends when one side is gone. Nothing is decided in a single tick, so a report that a world is *contested* is still true weeks later — and a relief force can arrive in time. **Landings.** Troops put down on a hostile world take guaranteed casualties on the way in unless enough marines land to form a **beachhead**; past that threshold the landing is clean and the ground contest begins at full strength. So marines do two jobs: they enforce an appointment, and they open a world for the army detachments behind them.

### UI

Two primary panes: **Inbox** (dispatches with origin/arrival stamps, reply and order actions, with a separate **Rumours** section for what the docks say) and **Map** (SVG hex subsector with lanes, last-known markers for ships and worlds carrying age badges; click for dossier). Secondary: **Roster**, **Outgoing** (dispatches queued on couriers), **Ledger**. One **Advance Week** button. Save/load as local JSON. **Orders are written in one dialog**, opened from a world ("send a hull here") or from a ship on the roster ("give orders"): which hull; where to send the order (default: where the desk last saw the hull, with its destination and rendezvous offered from the orders already sent, and — where no packet goes or a hull would be quicker — a courier in port to carry it); the destination; what to do on arrival (hold, patrol for N weeks, look for a week and report); disposition on contact; and where to go afterwards. The disposition and rally point travel with the order as standing orders.

On the map, **colour means allegiance** — who the player believes holds a world (loyal / unrest / revolt / independent / Warlord-held). **Age is the badge**: the coloured "as of N weeks ago" text above each marker, and nothing else — no fading or dimming of the marker itself. Lane schedules are read in the dossier ("packet every 2 weeks; next sails week 17") and on hover, not from line style.

## First campaign — "The Governor's Term"

The scenario Phases 1–2 are built to deliver. It is the default game; other seats (see *Phasing*, Phase 3) come later.

### Premise

The player is the **Subsector Governor**, holding supreme civil and military authority over the subsector on the Empire's behalf — and confined to an office in orbit above the **subsector capital**. Everything is done through subordinates, dispatches, and the hulls that carry them. The capital world itself has its own planetary governor, who commands the local troops and suppresses local unrest; the player governs the subsector, not the planet below.

The same is true of every world. The Empire does not run planets; it keeps **client rulers** — local governments that manage their own world and answer to the Empire for the spaceport and the trade fees, in the manner of the client kings of the old empires. A **garrison** is not an occupying army: it is enough to hold the **spaceport** and guard the **palace** of the client government. The *planetary governor* the player appoints is the Empire's resident at that court — the one who keeps the client in line, sees the fees collected, and writes home. That is why a garrison of a few detachments can hold a world of millions, why losing the garrison means losing the port and the resident rather than the population rising as one, and why a governor's letter is about the port, the palace, and the mood of the town around them. In this campaign the capital is always generated on the subsector's **edge**, nearest the border the Empire lies beyond, so the envoy's ship has a short, fixed approach.

### Starting position

Week 1 is already a crisis. Most worlds have inherited planetary governors of unknown loyalty. The Warlord has already defected and holds his first worlds. Some worlds are in unrest, and one or more have declared independence. Pirates are known to be raiding the lanes. The Council's envoy delivers the brief: **restore order.**

**Starting scale** (placeholders; the 1b playtest tunes them): the player has 8 warships — 4 J-2 patrol craft, 2 escorts, 2 transports — plus 6 scouts (there is no separate courier: the scout is the small fast unarmed hull that looks, watches and carries); 4 army and 2 marine detachments at the capital; a handful of officers without posts; and a treasury holding 12 months of upkeep. The Warlord holds 6 worlds in the far corner with 6 ships and scouts of his own. 4–6 pirate ships are at large.

### Goals

Two ways to win, and they pull against each other:

- **Loyal path — complete the term.** Serve out a fixed term of **208 weeks** without being replaced. Career advancement follows, graded by Council standing at the end (see *Endings*).
- **Rogue path — the pocket kingdom.** Declare the subsector independent. There is no threshold to meet; the declaration is a switch, and what happens next depends on who holds the seats. The declaration is itself news: it reaches each commander and governor by dispatch if the player sends one, otherwise by rumour, and each reacts on the week it reaches *them*. As the news arrives, they react by their dominant loyalty, shaded by temperament (*Characters*: bold ↔ cautious):
  - loyal to the *player* — keep following orders;
  - loyal to the *Empire* — turn against the player, boldly (attack, declare for the Empire, close ports) or cautiously (withdraw to a rally and await the expedition);
  - loyal to *themselves* — sit it out. Governors stop paying taxes and keep to themselves; ship officers hold their post, and the most daring turn pirate. They pledge to whoever wins once the outcome is evident.

  So the work of the rogue path is done before the declaration: appointing loyalists to the seats that matter (planetary governorships, ship captaincies, the capital's planetary governor) so the switch, when flipped, leaves the Empire's partisans outnumbered. From the declaration the Empire is an enemy: after an **unknown number of weeks** the Council assembles a fleet and sends it straight for the capital. **The first expedition ends the campaign**: the rogue path is won by holding the capital when it is defeated or turned back, and lost when it isn't. (A Phase 3 hand-off to the pocket-polity seat would begin with the self-loyal governors pledging to the player as news of the victory reaches them.)

The loyal path is Phase 1 scope. The rogue path needs the full loyalty model and is Phase 2 scope; Phase 1 should nonetheless track per-character loyalty so appointments made in Phase 1 games already carry weight.

### Endings

Every game ends with a ship in orbit over the capital. The Council's mood decides what the people aboard are for.

- **Recalled** (loss). Standing fell too far; the successor arrives with marines and the player steps down.
- **Captured** (loss). The capital's garrison is gone — to a rising or a landing — and the desk is taken with the palace: held for ransom by rebels, or paraded at the Warlord's show trial. Built in 1b as the one ending the prototype knows; the Warlord keeps his own seat garrisoned against the same fate.
- **Term completed** (win). Week 208: the same ship arrives, but this time the replacement is a colleague, and the player is carried back to the imperial core. High standing at the end earns a medal.
- **The parade** (best ending). The Warlord has been captured *and* the Council is **pleased** (the top standing tier, see *Threats → The Imperial Council*) when the term ends. Once the Warlord is in irons the Warlord front of every monthly report is good news, so a governor who has done it and then keeps the pirates down should get there.
- **Pocket kingdom** (rogue win). The subsector declared independent and the Empire's first expedition defeated or turned back at the capital.
- **Deposed** (rogue loss). The expedition takes the capital.

### Threats

Five sources of trouble. Each one reaches the player only as reports (Pillar 1) and is answered only by sending people and ships (Pillar 2).

- **The Warlord.** A former Commodore of the subsector fleet who defected, taking ships with him. He wants the whole subsector: enough ships, spaceports, and revenue to pay their upkeep, and then the capital — once strong enough, he comes for it. He grows by capturing worlds and by taking prizes, which he crews with officers he recruits at his seat. **He knows only what reaches him.** His belief state is tracked like the player's — built from the reports his governors and captains send to his capital, the scouts he sends out, and the talk that reaches his docks — and everything he decides is decided on that picture, which can be stale or wrong. Once a month, in this order: he **reinforces his own worlds** where his governor writes of revolt or a landing, where unrest is climbing, or where warships of the desk's have been seen nearby; he **lands** on the enemy world he believes he can take that would hurt the desk most to lose — chokepoints on the desk's routes to its capital, busy ports — sending troops enough to beat the garrison he believes is there and escorts enough to beat the warships his people saw, under the port's guns if they were docked, and never otherwise; he takes **independent worlds** when nothing of the desk's is within his means, or now and then for the port; he sends patrols after **enemy hulls he can take cheaply** — a courier at a weak port, a lone transport; and he sends his **scouts** first to a world of his whose governor has gone quiet or wrote of trouble, then to the enemy worlds he knows least about, the valuable ones first. His scouts are hulls the player's people may see, and a scout seen at a world is a warning that it is being looked at. **Pirates are his enemies too** — and his own corrupt governors breed them — so when he has ships to spare he clears the nests his people have seen, his own havens first; a pirate at a haven gets none of the port's guns, which are the holder's. He keeps an **officer pool** at his seat — a few officers without posts, rolled like anyone else but loyal to him or to themselves, one more recruited every eight weeks — and uses it to crew prizes and to **replace a governor** whose port his people have seen pirates lying at, sending the officer out with a marine. He does not yet tempt the desk's captains: **treason waits for money**, when bribes and fear can be built properly. He is the campaign's *rival polity* and its example of a *governor's secession* at once. Worlds and ships he captures go dark to the player until someone observes them again. There is no negotiating with him in this campaign (diplomacy is Phase 3); if the player goes rogue, he sends a taunt on hearing the news and carries on.
- **Pirates.** Raid the trade lanes, cutting revenue at A/B ports and sometimes taking tax convoys and packets (lost dispatches). They have no faction and no bases of their own: they rely on **havens** — worlds with a working spaceport whose planetary governor is corrupt enough to look the other way (including corrupt governors on Warlord-held worlds), or **independent worlds** with their port intact. Each week such a world has a **small** chance of spawning a new pirate ship; small enough that capturing or destroying the pirate ships is usually sufficient to keep piracy down. A pirate ship carries its own knowledge of havens, given at spawn (its home haven and whatever others it has heard of), and can only put in for repair and resupply at a world it *knows* to be a haven; a pirate that tries to land at any other port is captured by the local governor. So a pirate problem on a lane is usually also a *governor* problem — or an independence problem — at one end of it. The player does not know the havens: they are learned from rumour, from noticing where pirates come from and return to, and from **questioning captured crews** — a pirate taken in action or seized in port names each haven she knew on 2d6 ≥ 7, and on a 12 names a world that is no haven at all; the questioner writes it home as an ordinary letter, and the dossier lists what has been said about a world. Merchants complain about piracy, and merchants talk to the Empire.
- **Planetary governors.** (Placeholder title; better ones wanted.) In theory they answer to the Subsector Governor and are recalled at will. In practice a recall dispatch is a piece of paper. The default response of *any* governor recalled without marines — Empire-loyal ones included — is to ignore the message, stop paying taxes, and try to wait out the end of the player's term. Replacing a governor who does not want to go requires a **detachment of marines** arriving with the appointment. Their competence, loyalty, and self-interest (corruption is high *loyalty to self*, in the *Characters* model) decide how much tax arrives, how honest their reports are, and whether pirates find a welcome.
- **Unrest.** No single world here can project power or rival the Empire, even one that declares independence. But an unrestful world can blow up the planetary governor's office, stop paying taxes, and occupy the spaceport — closing the port to packets and fuel, and so to information. **Nobody knows a port has closed until word gets back**: packets keep sailing in and are impounded at the quay until the talk of the fall reaches the docks they sail from — along the lanes, at a merchant's pace, never faster — and the lane beyond a fallen world goes silent with it, because the post does not detour. Garrisons keep unrest from boiling over, and when it does they fight the rising week by week rather than losing at once (*Consequences → World states*); the answer is ships carrying **army and marine detachments**, which take weeks to arrive and may find the situation changed.
- **The Imperial Council** (the player's boss). Judges the player on what reaches the imperial capital: the player's own monthly report (see *Tribute and the envoy's visit*, below), **merchants' complaints** — mostly about piracy and other barriers to trade — and **rumour** (*Information model → Rumour propagation*), which travels slower than either. Trade routes out of the subsector cannot be closed to stop merchants talking without serious loss of revenue. The Council does *not* see who the player appoints or where loyalties lie, though specific appointments may displease it (an event: a noble's nephew arrives whom the player had better make a governor before promoting anyone else). **Standing** moves on what the player *says* each month, on whether the tribute arrived, and on merchants' complaints; rumour never moves it directly — rumour only shapes what the Council believes to be true, and it is being *caught* in a contradiction that costs (numbers under *Council standing*, below). Let standing fall too far and the Council replaces the player: the loyal path is lost. **There is no recall letter.** The recall *is* the successor: a new governor arriving at the capital aboard a ship carrying a detachment of marines, who **takes over in the same tick he arrives**. By the time an unknown ship is seen in the system, it is already too late. The only warning is rumour — word of how pleased or displeased the Council is, second-hand and weeks old — and the player uses it to judge how much danger they are in. A player who means to go rogue must anticipate the removal and declare first. If the declaration is made while the successor is *en route*, he arrives at a hostile capital: the player's forces can defeat his marines and capture his ship. The ship becomes a prize (see *Consequences → Prizes*); the marines do not. The Council learns of it no faster than of anything else — a captured ship sends no report. On the rogue path, the Council is the enemy that sends the fleet.

### Tribute and the envoy's visit

Taxes owed to the Empire travel physically. Every month the **Council's envoy** arrives at the capital aboard the **tribute delegation** — a specific ship with a specific character aboard; the player hands over the subsector's taxes and gives the monthly report that the Council will eventually read. The delegation then carries both back out of the subsector. Whether it needs escorting depends on the map: a capital on the subsector's edge is a short hop from the border, one in the centre means the delegation crosses pirate country. A lost delegation is lost money, a missing report, and — when the merchants get there first — bad news.

The envoy is the **same character every month** — a relationship the player can cultivate or sour. The visit is an **event with choices**: the report has two sections, *the war against the Warlord* and *the war against the pirates*, and in each the player says one of two things:

- *"Under control."* — Standing rises a little now. But the Council keeps, per topic, two beliefs: **reported truth** (set by the player's reports) and **rumoured truth** (moved, with some chance, by each rumour of a victory, a defeat, a raid, a world lost or retaken that reaches the imperial capital). Where the two contradict, there is a chance the Council decides the player is a liar: standing drops hard, and at the next visit the envoy's reprimand quotes the rumours — *"You said the Warlord was contained; he destroyed the* Vigilant *above Harrow, and pirates took two merchants off Kesh."* Because only some rumours travel, and bad news travels best, a player who is genuinely doing well can still be caught out by a Council that heard only the defeats.
- *"It is a disaster; send help."* — Standing falls a little, and the player may **request aid** for that topic: troops, ships, officers, or funding. One request per topic reported as a disaster, so at most two a visit. The Council grants by mood (a pleased Council always, an alarmed one never, the tiers between on a roll). A granted request delivers **two** of what was asked — 2 ships, 2 detachments, 2 officers, or 6 months of upkeep — arriving with the delegation *after* the one that carried the request home, so some two to three months later. The string: it comes with a Council-chosen, Empire-loyal officer the player is expected to place. The player may also **offer extra tribute** — a surcharge due with the next delegation — to buy one more unit on any granted request; miss the surcharge and it counts as tribute short.

The envoy sometimes — not always — lets slip how the Council currently feels about the player, which is the freshest reading of Council mood the player can get, though still weeks old. Other events may ride in with the visit (the noble's nephew, a quota change, a censure).

### Council standing

A hidden integer, 0–20, starting at **12**. The player never sees it; they see a **tier** — *alarmed* (0–4), *displeased* (5–8), *satisfied* (9–12), *pleased* (13–20) — and only through rumour or the envoy's candour. All numbers are placeholders for the 1c playtest; the *invariants* are what matter.

| Each monthly visit | Standing |
|---|---|
| A topic reported "under control" | +1 |
| A topic reported "a disaster" | −1 |
| Tribute on time and in full | +1 |
| Tribute short or late | −1 |
| Tribute missing (delegation lost, nothing handed over) | −2 |
| Merchants complaining (piracy on the border lanes above a threshold this month) | −1 |
| **Caught lying** on a topic (see below) | **−5**, and *liar* is remembered |

Rumours reaching the imperial capital move that topic's **rumoured truth** and nothing else. At each visit, per topic where reported truth is *good* and rumoured truth is *bad*, the Council rolls the **liar check**: 2d6 ≥ 8 catches the player; while *liar* is remembered (three visits) it is 2d6 ≥ 6. A caught lie also resets that topic's reported truth to bad, so the +1 was for nothing.

Invariants the numbers must keep:

- **Being caught is worse than three months of honest failure** (−5 vs −1 a month).
- **A lie has negative expected value whenever the rumours are against you** (+1 with the odds it isn't caught, −5 when it is: at 2d6 ≥ 8 that is about −1.5, worse than the honest −1), and the *liar* memory makes the second lie worse than the first.
- **Honesty never raises standing** — there is no bonus for confessing — and a truthful "disaster" while rumour says all is well costs nothing beyond the −1.
- A player doing nothing (two disasters a month, tribute intact) goes from satisfied to alarmed in about four months; a mixed month (one topic good, one bad) holds level.

**Recall** fires when the player is *alarmed* at two consecutive visits: the successor departs the imperial capital on that week and takes the border-lane transit (see *The Empire's reaction clock*). The Council's mood at each visit is what rumour of "how the Council feels" carries back into the subsector.

### Consequences for the model

Things this campaign requires beyond what *Requirements* already lists:

- **Troops.** Army detachments (garrison, hold a world) and marine detachments (boarding, seizing a spaceport or a governor's office, and **landing on a hostile world** — enough of them form a beachhead that spares the force behind them its casualties on the way in; see *Combat*) as transportable units with strength, carried by ships under the *Transport* order. A **transport lifts three detachments; a warship one** in her spare berths; a scout none. Troops travel **cryofrozen** between worlds, and a standard **10%** do not survive revival — so a detachment arrives weaker than it left, by an amount the sender learns only from the commander's report on arrival. Moving troops is therefore costly in itself, and stationing them ahead of need beats rushing them in.
- **Appointments travel.** An appointment is a dispatch; it takes effect when it arrives — and, for an unwilling incumbent, when the marines do.
- **Loyalty as a resource.** Each seat records who holds it and where their loyalty runs (player / Empire / self). Nothing gates the declaration; loyalty and temperament decide which way each seat falls once the news of it arrives there. This needs a *neutral* allegiance — a seat obeying nobody during a succession crisis — so `Faction` allegiance cannot be a two-value enum.
- **The Council's ears.** Council **standing** is a hidden value (*Council standing*, above), moved by the player's monthly report, by tribute arriving on time, and by merchant complaints. Rumour feeds only the Council's per-topic *rumoured truth*; contradiction with the *reported truth* is what triggers the liar check. Standing leaks back to the player only through rumour and the envoy's occasional candour.
- **The imperial capital is a node beyond the border.** It sits off the map, joined to the subsector capital by a **border lane** with a fixed transit of several weeks. The tribute delegation, the successor, the expedition, aid, and rumour in both directions all traverse that one lane, so everything the Empire does or hears obeys the same timetable as everything else.
- **The Empire's reaction clock.** The successor and the punitive expedition are ships that depart the imperial capital on a hidden week, driven by what the Council has *received*, and take the border-lane transit to arrive. They are visible only if some hull observes them en route and gets word back first.
- **Events and channels.** The `Event` record and the `channel` on every report, specified under *Information model*. The player's inbox shows official mail and rumour as two separate piles.
- **A traffic log per world.** The event record is it: ground truth keeps every event at every world — each hull's arrival and departure among them — for longer than any watch, so a scout's watch report and a governor's letter are drawn from the same record, one of them faithfully.
- **Scouts.** Scout ships as a hull class with the escape bonus; the *Scout* order with a stay in weeks, a look at one and a watch beyond it. The Warlord's belief state and scouts are the same machinery pointed the other way.
- **World states.** A world can be loyal, unrestful, in revolt, independent, or Warlord-held, and the player's belief about which may be wrong. **Revolt is a contest, not a switch.** When unrest boils over the world enters *revolt*: the event triggers a governor's letter asking for help (all but the most desperate write; the disclosure roll applies) and seeds rumours on the lanes. Taxes fall sharply or stop while the world is in conflict. A world with **no garrison** falls at once. Otherwise the garrison fights it out under the ground-combat model (*Combat*, above): each week there is a chance the garrison loses a detachment to ambushes and assaults, and each week the garrison may put the rising down. If the garrison reaches zero, the governor **flees to a ship in orbit** if there is one and is **killed** if there is not; the world is then *independent* — office gone, port closed to packets and fuel, and so to mail. An **independent world projects no power** — it builds no ships and threatens no neighbour — but it slowly raises a **garrison of its own**, so the longer it is left the more troops it takes to bring back, and its intact port makes it a pirate haven meanwhile. The same contest, with the Warlord's troops in the rebels' place, is how worlds change hands between the player and him.
- **Rumour.** Merchant-borne news along the lanes; specified under *Information model*.
- **Prizes.** A ship *captured* in combat (pirate, Warlord, or the successor's) sails under a **prize crew** to the captor's rendezvous — the rally point in the captor's standing orders, the capital by default — and lies there until an officer is sent out to take command (by *Transport*). A ship *destroyed* is unsalvageable. Prizes are the main way the fleet grows in this campaign. **Packets need no officer**: a nameless junior takes any packet, so a captured or impounded packet runs for her new holder wherever both ends of her lane are theirs, and lies idle otherwise. The Warlord takes prizes too, and recruits officers at his seat to crew them. Pirates who take an *armed* hull make her a pirate on the spot, with her captor's havens; unarmed hulls they only rob.
- **Bonus pay.** Significant extra pay to a captain or governor can flip their loyalty to the player — most readily for the self-interested, who were only ever for sale. It is the peaceful complement to marines, and drains the treasury the tribute delegation is waiting for.
- **Fleet command.** The Commodore's seat the Warlord vacated is not refilled: the player appoints ship captains directly and commands ship by ship, so every captaincy is a loyalty decision the player owns.
- **Conversation.** One officer per week at the capital; specified under *Characters → Learning them*. This is the player's only way to read a character's loyalty before appointing them, so the seat-stacking game of the rogue path runs through it.

### Open questions

None at present. The twelve raised while building 1b were settled on 2026-09-20 (decisions.md, *Settling the 1b questions*) and folded into *Combat*, *Threats → Pirates*, *Endings*, *Consequences → Troops* and *Prizes*, and *Information model*.

**Standing principle:** an event records whom it went *against* and whom it *favoured*, and every reader — a governor deciding whether to write, the docks deciding whether to talk — judges it from their own side, so the Warlord's people see his losses as losses and the desk's as good news. The choices subordinates make about what to write, when, and to whom must stay realistic for who they are and whom they serve; revisit whenever a new kind of subordinate decision is added.

## Architecture

- `src/sim/` — pure TypeScript, deterministic, no DOM. Subsector generation, entities, orders, movement, courier and report propagation, combat, events, character behaviour. Seeded PRNG; whole state serializable to JSON.
- `src/ui/` — React + SVG. Reads a `PlayerView` derived solely from delivered reports; never touches ground truth except through a dev-only "god view" toggle.
- The sim/ui boundary is strict: the UI cannot cheat, so the belief model is enforced by structure rather than discipline.
- Tests (Vitest) on the sim: determinism (same seed → same state), propagation scenarios (a report from world X arrives at week D + transit), order resolution, and character-trait effects.

## Data model / schemas

Sketch; formalized in `src/sim/types.ts` as it lands.

- `World` — hex coordinate, name, profile (starport, size, atmosphere, hydrographics, population, government, law, tech), faction, governor, unrest, garrison.
- `Lane` — two worlds, jump distance, packet schedule.
- `Ship` — class, jump rating, cargo, crew, commander, true position, current order, standing orders, own belief state.
- `Character` — attributes, traits, post, relationships, career history.
- `Order` — typed record with parameters and rendezvous.
- `Dispatch` — from, to-address, payload (order, appointment, letter), sent week, route, eta, held-at.
- `Event` — where, when, kind, subject, valence, severity. The thing governors mention, rumours degrade, and the Council folds over.
- `Report` — subject, observed-at location, observed week, delivered week, snapshot (a world, a ship, or an event), observer, **channel** (`official` / `agent` / `merchant` / `docks`). A rumour is a `Report` on the `merchant` or `docks` channel; there is no fidelity number.
- `Faction` — the Empire, the player's administration, rivals, pirates, rebels. Allegiance of a seat is player / Empire / self / **neutral** (sitting out a succession), never a two-value flag.
- `GameState` — tick, ground truth, per-agent belief states, player view, RNG state.

## Phasing

- **Phase 0 — Belief map.** Subsector generation, lanes, packet schedules, couriers, reports, hex map with age badges. No ships to command, no combat. Goal: prove that watching stale information arrive is interesting on its own.
- **Phase 1 — The desk.** Ships and orders, patrols, pirates, governors with basic traits, troops, the inbox, Empire demands, the Warlord. A complete, winnable/losable loop: the *loyal path* of the first campaign. Built in three cuts, each ending in a playtest: **1a — Hulls and people** (warships, scouts, events, selective governors, rumour); **1b — Trouble** (world states, pirates, combat, troops, the Warlord, agents); **1c — The Empire** (economy, the border lane, tribute and the envoy, standing, recall, endings).
- **Phase 2 — People.** Full character traits and relationships, deputies, player mobility (leaving the capital, with a deputy holding the seal), ambition and usurpation — including the first campaign's *rogue path*.
- **Phase 3 — Other seats.** Pocket polities and a rogue admiral as playable roles; richer economy; diplomacy.
